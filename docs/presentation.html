The problem

A shareholder register lists companies as often as people

  • The company's register names another company. That company's register names a third. The controller is at the top.
  • Effective stake multiplies along the chain: 37.69% × 43.74% = 16.49%.
  • Names drift between filings, companies hold each other in loops, and the top of the chain is often a company that files nothing.
  • Goal: resolve the chain, find the loops, measure control.

Chains · Phase 4

DFS with an explicit stack recovers every chain

  • Adjacency lists: 405 nodes, 946 edges, density 0.58%.
  • One stack frame per company on the current chain, so the whole chain can be read off the stack.
  • A chain stops at a natural person, an owner with no recorded owner, a loop, or a minimum effective stake.
  • Chains are ranked by effective stake; routes to the same owner are summed.

Control through loops · Phase 6

Do the loops hide control? No.

Inside each circular group, every route round the loop is summed with the stake matrix A:

x = (I + A + A² + A³ + …) · b

Computed by repeated matrix multiplication (at most 11 × 11); elsewhere, one pass in Tarjan's order.

Conclusion

Control is concentrated and shallow. The data stops before the people do.

  • One unlisted holding company per group controls almost every company, one or two layers up.
  • Cross-holdings are real (an 11-company Tata loop) but too weak to hide control.
  • What hides the people is the unlisted holding company whose owners these filings do not disclose, not the depth of layering.

Limits

  • About 57% of each company is traced; holders below 1% are unnamed.
  • Integrated stake is economic interest, not votes.
  • Entity-resolution rules were fitted to this data; no held-out set.
